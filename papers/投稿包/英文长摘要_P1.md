## Extended Abstract

In lexicographic practice, prototype judgments typically remain implicit intuitions underlying sense selection: which senses enter a study, which count as central, and which are discarded are decisions grounded in trained intuition and editorial convention rather than in explicit criteria. Such judgments are neither reviewable — peers cannot check whether a uniform standard was applied — nor traceable — no evidence chain survives the moment of decision. The problem is amplified when the object of study is an entire word class rather than a single entry. This paper, the first of a series on the schema-based categorization of English motion verbs, addresses the problem by turning Rosch's prototype theory into an operational, quantifiable, reviewable and traceable adjudication procedure at the level of the individual sense.

The empirical basis is a lemma pool of 216 candidates drawn from Levin's (1993) Chapter 51, reduced to 171 lemmas (136 manner-focusing, 22 path-focusing, 13 interaction-focusing) after Talmy-style semantic verification and Levin-style alternation checks, with all 45 rejections individually documented. Sense-level material comes from OALD10: 996 sense units (992 OALD senses plus four manually supplemented senses for lemmas lacking verbal entries). The method has three components. First, an admission trichotomy — Y (core physical-displacement senses), Extension (metaphorical projections retaining a spatial-displacement schema), N (excluded senses, fully archived with mandatory reasons) — is codified in a standardized coding manual with three boundary arbitration clauses and a forced reason field for every label. Second, a three-table workflow organizes the data lineage: Table 1 as a read-only archive of all 996 senses; Table 2 as the judgment layer where rows are labeled but never deleted; Table 3 as the final sense list extracted automatically from human-reviewed flags on the MOVECAT annotation platform. Third, a dual-track pipeline couples mechanical regular-expression screening (992 → 358 candidates, using ten positive and seventeen negative signal classes), LLM pre-screening with a fixed prompt (162 verb files covering 352 senses; outputs archived in full and treated as candidates only) and exhaustive human review, with a reliability design of 15%–20% stratified sampling to be scored by Cohen's kappa or Krippendorff's alpha, falling back to intra-coder retest where no second coder is available.

Pilot results from a 12-verb sample (232 senses) quantify why mechanical filtering alone cannot decide admission: after rule-based screening, 92 idiomatic senses were pre-labeled N, 88 ambiguous-voice senses remained to be confirmed, 49 transitive senses required case-by-case judgment and 3 lacked grammatical codes — nearly sixty percent of the pilot material still awaiting human disposition. Layered case analyses of the LLM pre-screening output (limp, charge, race) show that the value of machine screening lies less in label accuracy than in rendering reasons and doubts explicit: the limp case surfaced a register choice in Chinese that flips the alignment grade, the charge case anticipated a directional-complement issue, and the race case exposed a competition-semantics doubt at the very core of the admission decision. The version history of the lemma list — narrowed to 136 manner verbs in an earlier revision and restored to 171 — is analyzed as a methodological case of reflexive boundary correction: because every step was documented, the reversal is defensible rather than capricious.

The procedure yields three contributions: a rule-by-rule operationalization of prototype theory for sense-level lexicographic work; a data structure that makes screening reproducible and auditable; and a quality-control template for LLM-assisted annotation in the humanities, in which the model drafts and flags doubts while humans adjudicate and the system preserves evidence.

**Keywords**: prototype theory; sense unit; admission trichotomy; three-table workflow; LLM-assisted annotation; coding reliability; learners' dictionaries
